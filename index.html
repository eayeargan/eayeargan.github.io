Case Study

Preventing $24M in annual transaction failures through SQL-driven diagnosis.

How I used transaction data to identify a structural mismatch between authorization windows and rental contract durations — and resolved it with a single merchant account change.

Context

Dealerware is a B2B SaaS platform serving automotive fleet operators. As the lead PM owning payments, invoicing, and merchant onboarding, I had full authority over the payment product surface — and full accountability for when it broke. When I joined the team, the merchant account was recording a persistently high volume of “unsuccessful” transactions. The number was large enough to be alarming, but nobody had yet dug into what was driving it or what it was costing the business annually.

Phase 01 — Problem Definition
First question: what is actually failing?
Before proposing any fix, I needed to understand the structure of the failure. I queried the transaction data to find the most prevalent status code across all unsuccessful transactions. The answer was unambiguous: “authorization expired” was the dominant failure type by a wide margin. This immediately reframed the problem. We weren’t dealing with declines, fraud flags, or network errors — we were dealing with pre-authorizations that were being captured too late. The authorization was dying before the transaction could complete.
“Authorization expired” → dominant failure status Declines & network errors ruled out
Phase 02 — Investigation
The timing told the whole story.
Knowing authorizations were expiring was the start, not the answer. The next question was why — specifically, whether the expiry window was structurally incompatible with how Dealerware’s rental contracts actually worked. I queried the average duration between a transaction being authorized and reaching an expired status, then compared that against the average rental contract duration (removing outliers for a clean signal). The result was clear: pre-authorizations were expiring before rental contracts had a chance to complete and capture. That led me to the merchant account configuration itself, where I found our merchant had been assigned an MCC (Merchant Category Code) permitting only a 7-day authorization hold period — far too short for a vehicle rental business where contracts routinely run longer.
Avg auth duration vs avg contract duration compared Outliers removed for clean signal MCC → 7-day auth limit identified
Phase 03 — Decision
One change. The right change.
The fix was precise because the diagnosis was precise. I had our merchant account code changed to “vehicle rental” — the MCC classification that accurately reflected what Dealerware’s customers were doing, and which afforded the maximum authorization hold period permitted across all major card networks. No infrastructure work. No sprint planning. No cross-team coordination. The elegance of the outcome came entirely from asking the right questions of the data before touching anything.
MCC changed to “vehicle rental” Maximum auth period unlocked No engineering work required
Phase 04 — Outcome
The volume of unsuccessful transactions crashed.
With authorization periods now aligned to actual rental contract durations, pre-authorizations stopped expiring before they could be captured. The volume of unsuccessful transactions dropped sharply. Annualized, the transaction value that had previously been resolving as “authorization expired” — and was now completing successfully — came to approximately $24M. To be precise: this wasn’t revenue lost to fraud or chargebacks. These were valid transactions with real customers failing purely because the authorization window was misconfigured relative to the contract lifecycle. The MCC change prevented them from failing.
Unsuccessful transaction volume crashed $24M in transactions annually now completing Root cause: auth window vs contract duration mismatch
Phase 05 — Retrospective
What I’d do differently.
The MCC mismatch had likely been present since the merchant account was first configured — well before I joined. Every month it went unexamined was another month of valid transactions failing quietly. The lesson: authorization expiry rate should be a standing KPI with an alert threshold from day one, not something you investigate when volume gets impossible to ignore. I’ve since made “unsuccessful transaction rate by failure status” a mandatory instrumentation requirement in every payment-adjacent product I’ve owned. The second thing I’d do differently: I would have quantified the annual dollar impact at discovery and brought that number to the CFO before making the change — not after. The data supported treating this as a business emergency. I treated it as a product task instead.
