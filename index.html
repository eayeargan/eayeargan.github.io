Case Study

Recovering $24M in annual revenue through transaction data interrogation.

How I used SQL-driven analysis to identify the root cause of payment failures at Dealerware — and what the decision process looked like from problem to outcome.

Context

Dealerware is a B2B SaaS platform serving automotive fleet operators. As the sole PM owning payments, contracting, and merchant onboarding, I had full authority over the payment product surface — and full accountability for when it broke. In late 2022, we had a persistent and quietly expensive problem: a meaningful percentage of transactions were failing silently, and nobody had a precise number for what it was costing us.

Phase 01 — Problem Definition
The signal was hiding in plain sight.
The engineering team knew there were transaction failures. What nobody had done was quantify the pattern. My first step wasn't to write a spec — it was to write a query. I pulled 90 days of transaction logs and started looking for structure in the noise: failure rates by merchant category, time of day, card network, transaction size band, and device type. What I found was that the failures weren't random. Three specific failure codes accounted for 78% of the volume, and two of them were concentrated in a single merchant segment.
90-day transaction dataset 5 failure dimensions analyzed 3 codes → 78% of failures
Phase 02 — Investigation
The root cause wasn't what engineering expected.
The working hypothesis from the team was a network timeout issue — a plausible guess that would have led to an expensive infrastructure change. The data told a different story. The concentrated failure pattern pointed to a misconfiguration in the merchant category code (MCC) mapping for a subset of fleet accounts, which was causing certain card networks to reject transactions at the authorization stage before any network issue could even occur. The infrastructure hypothesis would have solved nothing. I brought the SQL output to a joint session with Engineering and our payment processor's integration team and walked through the data structure. The processor confirmed the MCC mismatch within the hour.
Root cause: MCC mapping error Infrastructure rebuild averted
Phase 03 — Decision & Tradeoffs
Two fixes available. I chose the slower one.
There were two paths: a fast patch that would manually correct the affected accounts within a week, and a systematic fix that would rebuild the MCC mapping logic at the data layer to prevent recurrence for any future merchant onboarding. The fast patch would have closed the immediate bleeding in 5 days. The systematic fix would take 3 weeks and require coordination with two engineering squads. I chose the systematic fix. The math was straightforward — the fast patch left the root cause intact, meaning any new merchant onboarded through the same flow would recreate the problem within months. The 3-week cost was a one-time investment against a recurring revenue leak. What I decided not to do: I explicitly did not bundle this with a broader "payments infrastructure refresh" that was on the roadmap. Scope creep would have pushed delivery to 8 weeks and introduced unrelated risk.
Systematic fix chosen over patch Scope contained deliberately 3-week delivery
Phase 04 — Outcome
65% reduction in failures. $24M recovered annually.
Within 60 days of shipping, transaction failure rates had dropped 65% across the affected merchant segment. Annualized, the recovered revenue from transactions that would otherwise have failed silently came to approximately $24M. No new infrastructure. No platform migration. A correct diagnosis and a deliberate build decision. The monitoring query I wrote for this investigation became a standing dashboard that the Customer Success team still uses to catch configuration drift on new merchant onboardings.
65% failure rate reduction $24M annual revenue recovered Monitoring dashboard still live
Phase 05 — Retrospective
What I'd do differently.
The MCC mismatch had been present for at least 8 months before I ran the query. The cost of that delay was real — roughly $16M in revenue that failed silently before the fix shipped. The lesson I took from it: payment failure rates should be a standing KPI with an alert threshold, not a metric you check when something feels wrong. I've since made "silent failure rate by error code" a mandatory instrumentation requirement in every payment-adjacent product I've owned. The second thing I'd do differently: I would have escalated the financial magnitude of the problem to the CFO and CEO at the discovery stage, not after the fix was live. The data supported an emergency priority decision — I treated it as a standard roadmap item instead.
